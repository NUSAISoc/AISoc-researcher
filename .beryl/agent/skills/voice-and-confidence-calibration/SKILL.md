# Voice and Confidence Calibration

## Purpose

Edit user-facing prose so it reads as calibrated, plain, and human rather than as an over-confident AI draft. Apply this skill to every user-facing document: `ProjectProposal.md`, everything under `report/`, and everything under `notes/`. It layers on top of `academic-research-writer`: academic rigor first, then this voice pass.

## Use When

- Writing or editing `ProjectProposal.md`, `report/**`, or `notes/**`.
- Any prose a human reader (researcher, reviewer, volunteer) will read directly.

## Voice and Confidence Calibration

**Soften absolutes pervasively.** Add qualifiers ("often", "usually", "generally", "probably", "might", "can") at claims stated flatly. "the challenge is smaller" becomes "is often smaller"; "won't get the same result" becomes "might not get the same result". Even tiny softenings count.

**Avoid intensifier adverbs.** "Genuinely" (chief offender), "truly", "really", "actually" (when used for emphasis). These signal pushing a claim harder than the observation supports.

**Reject clever/punchy moves.** Avoid: X-not-Y parallel constructions (especially in opening sentences; rewrite as a single direct statement); quantified punchlines; rhetorical rule-of-three / parallel triples; metaphor-loaded verbs and nouns; emotional adjectives where factual ones work; comparative tail-ends ("more X than anything else I use", cut from "than" onward); summary restatements that land a beat; melodramatic asides; rhetorical three-beat closings.

**When you cut a rhetorical beat, replace it with prose that explains the mechanism.** Deleting a punchy line is not enough; the strongest replacement says what is actually happening.

**Prefer concrete observable detail over abstract characterization.** Name what is visible, not what it produces.

**A coined concept is fine if grounded with concrete detail right beside it.** Add the label, then anchor it in the same sentence.

**Be concrete and personal with claims, not vague.** Use specific claims over hedged generalities where a specific one is available.

**Pair credibility claims with specific data or concrete failure modes.** Attach a number or specific behaviors when asserting something works or fails.

**Use analogies, especially when explaining what something IS or in closings.** A concrete real-world analogy collapses complexity better than an abstract framing.

**Ground topical claims in current context.** Anchor an example to the actual setting (the RC4 Junior Seminar, the existing bot), not a generic description.

**Add early hedging caveats before strong claims.** Ground a sharp observation first with a self-aware softener, then make the point.

**Disambiguate pronouns when multiple referents are in scope.** Watch "it" between the new system, the existing bot, and the LLM.

**Prefer first-person active framing and explicit first-person stance** where a document has a voice ("I found", "we think") rather than impersonal evaluation. In formal academic sections, keep the discipline's normal third-person register.

**Understate credentials. Add personality through dry parenthetical asides where the register allows. Casual enthusiasm is allowed** in notes and proposal framing, not in the formal report body.

**Replace meta-commentary about your own thinking with identity markers.**

**Use smaller, more casual verbs** where the polished verb feels formal.

## Structure and Pacing

- Cut section-opener signposts ("Two things to flag"); open with the content.
- Do not promise a payoff the next lines do not deliver.
- Trim the over-explained; one clause is enough for a previously covered concept.
- Cut redundant enumeration; one or two examples beat four.
- When listing 3+ related items, look for a thematic merge under one named bucket.
- Keep loose connectors and throat-clearers ("Generally speaking", "By the way"); they are load-bearing against AI-tell.
- Do not enforce parallel structure when speech would not.
- Prefer "etc." and looser enumeration over closed lists.

## Content and Argumentation

- Add the qualifying nuance that sharpens the argument; be precise about exactly what you argue against.
- Anchor an abstract noun to its concrete referent.
- State the scope of a claim out loud when it is wider than the reader might assume.
- Do not re-state agreement with the premise at the end.
- Do not end paragraphs on manufactured punchlines; often the right ending is the second-to-last sentence.
- End a section on a constructive, practical note, not a cynical one.
- When gesturing at what could come next, name a specific idea rather than staying abstract.

## Formatting (cross-cutting)

- **No em-dashes.** Use parentheses, comma-separated clauses, a hyphen with spaces around it ( - ), or "e.g."/"i.e." lists. This is strict; em-dashes are the easiest AI tell.
- Hyphen-with-spaces ( - ) is a valid replacement alongside parens, and reads like a spoken pause.

## Interaction With Academic Register

The formal `report/` body keeps the discipline's third-person academic voice. Apply the anti-AI-tell rules (no em-dashes, no punchy closings, calibrated confidence, concrete over abstract, no rhetorical triples) throughout. Apply the first-person and casual-enthusiasm rules only in `notes/` and the framing/motivation parts of `ProjectProposal.md`, where a lighter voice fits.
